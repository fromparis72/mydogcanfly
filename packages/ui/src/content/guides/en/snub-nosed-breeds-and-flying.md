If you own a French bulldog, a pug or a boxer, you have probably already met a booking refusal, or a policy page that stops short of a clear answer. The restrictions applied to snub-nosed — **brachycephalic** — breeds are among the strictest in pet air travel, and they are also among the most misunderstood. They are not a commercial preference. They exist because these dogs are measurably less able to cope with heat and stress, and a cargo hold concentrates both.

## What "brachycephalic" actually means for a flight

A brachycephalic dog has a shortened skull and muzzle. The soft tissue of the airway is not shortened to the same degree, so the airway is more crowded: narrower nostrils, a longer soft palate, a narrower windpipe in many individuals.

That matters in the air because **a dog cools itself almost entirely by panting**. Panting moves air fast over moist surfaces; a crowded airway makes that work harder and less effective. Now add what a flight actually involves — waiting on a hot apron, engine noise, vibration, being handled by strangers, no water for a period — and the margin that exists at home disappears.

This is why the restriction so often applies to the **hold** specifically, and why it tightens in summer. Our guide to [summer heat embargoes](/travel-hub/pet-heat-embargoes/) covers the temperature side, which compounds this one.

## Which dogs are concerned

Every airline publishes its own list, and the lists genuinely differ. The breeds that appear on nearly all of them include the [French bulldog](/breeds/french-bulldog/), the [English bulldog](/breeds/english-bulldog/), the [pug](/breeds/pug/), the [boxer](/breeds/boxer/), the [Boston terrier](/breeds/boston-terrier/) and the [shih tzu](/breeds/shih-tzu/).

Two points travellers often miss:

- **Crosses count.** A cross with a visibly shortened muzzle is usually treated as brachycephalic, whatever the paperwork says.
- **The trait is what is assessed.** Some carriers write their rule around the muzzle rather than a list, which means a dog not named anywhere can still be refused at the desk.

## What the restrictions look like in practice

They come in several forms, and it is worth knowing which one you are facing:

- **Total refusal in the hold**, year-round. The most common.
- **Seasonal refusal**, tied to temperature thresholds at any point on the route.
- **Cabin only**, subject to the usual size and weight limits.
- **Cabin restricted too**, on a minority of carriers.
- **Acceptance with a veterinary certificate**, sometimes with a larger crate than the size alone would require.

Because the variation is so wide, this is a case where a general answer is useless. Compare the carriers serving your route on our [airline pet policy pages](/airlines/), then confirm directly — and re-confirm close to departure, for the reasons set out in [why airline pet policies change](/travel-hub/airline-pet-policy-changes/).

## If the cabin is possible, prefer it

For a small brachycephalic dog, the cabin is not merely more comfortable — it is the option where you can see the animal, where the temperature is regulated for humans, and where you can act if something goes wrong. If your dog is within the size limits, treat the cabin as the objective and build the booking around it, starting with a carrier that fits under the seat: see [the most comfortable carriers for small dogs](/travel-hub/small-dog-carriers/) and our [cabin carrier selection](/tools/best-carriers/).

Give the dog time to accept the carrier well before departure. A brachycephalic dog that panics in an enclosed bag is in a worse position than one that treats it as a den — our guide to [dog travel anxiety](/travel-hub/dog-travel-anxiety/) covers the habituation, which needs weeks rather than days.

## If the answer is no

It sometimes is, and the honest options are limited: manifest cargo where the carrier accepts it, a different carrier or season, or not flying. What is **not** an option is misdeclaring the breed. It is caught often, it voids the arrangement, and it puts the dog in exactly the situation the rule exists to prevent.

Before deciding either way, ask a veterinarian who has examined your dog. Brachycephalic dogs vary enormously between individuals: some cope well, some are already breathing at their limit at rest. That assessment is worth more than any policy page — and if the answer is that your dog should not fly, our guides to [travelling by car](/travel-hub/traveling-by-car-with-a-dog/) and [by train](/travel-hub/train-travel-with-a-dog/) cover what remains.
