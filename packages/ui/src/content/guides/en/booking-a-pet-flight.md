Booking a flight for yourself is a two-minute transaction. Booking one for your dog is not the same exercise at all, and the mistake that costs the most is treating the animal as an option you add at the end. On most airlines, a pet is a **separate reservation**, with its own quota, its own fee and its own confirmation — and that reservation can be refused long after your own seat is paid for.

This guide sets out the order that actually works, and the checks that keep a booking from falling apart at the desk. For the wider picture of what flying with a dog involves, start with our overview of [flying with a dog](/travel-hub/flying-with-a-dog/).

## Why the pet booking comes first

Cabin spots are capped per flight, and the cap is low — often a handful of animals for the whole aircraft, sometimes fewer in a given cabin class. Those places go early, especially on summer routes and around holidays. The hold has its own limits, tied to the aircraft type and to the temperature rules in force on the day.

So the sequence matters:

1. Read the pet policy of the airline you intend to fly, on its own page rather than a summary.
2. Confirm a spot is available on your specific flight, for your specific animal.
3. Only then buy your ticket.
4. Get the pet confirmation in writing.

Our [comparison of airline pet policies](/airlines/) puts the conditions of more than a hundred carriers side by side, which is the fastest way to see whether a route is workable before you spend anything.

## What the airline will ask you

Have these to hand before you call. The questions are the same almost everywhere:

- **The animal's weight**, and on most airlines the weight of the dog *and* the carrier together.
- **The carrier or crate dimensions**, in centimetres or inches.
- **The breed** — some are restricted or refused in the hold, a point we cover in our guide to [snub-nosed breeds and flying](/travel-hub/snub-nosed-breeds-and-flying/).
- **The route and the aircraft**, because a connection may put your pet under two different sets of rules.

If you do not yet know the crate size you need, size it before you call rather than guessing: our [travel crate calculator](/tools/crate/) and the guide to [measuring your dog for a crate](/travel-hub/measuring-your-dog-for-a-crate/) give you a figure you can quote.

## Cabin, hold or cargo: decide before you call

The three options are not variants of one another — they are three different contracts, with different prices and different paperwork. The full comparison is in our guide to [cabin, hold and cargo](/travel-hub/flying-with-a-dog-cabin-hold-cargo/), and the commercial differences in [cabin, excess baggage or manifest cargo](/travel-hub/cargo-vs-excess-baggage/).

In short: a small dog that fits under the seat travels with you; a larger animal travels in the hold as accompanied baggage when the airline allows it; and on routes or breeds where neither is possible, manifest cargo is the remaining route. Knowing which one you are asking for makes the call much shorter.

## Get it in writing, and re-check

A verbal confirmation is worth very little at four in the morning at a check-in desk. Ask for the confirmation to appear **on the booking itself**, and keep an email that states the flight, the travel mode, the accepted dimensions and the total weight.

Then re-check. Two things routinely change between booking and departure:

- **The aircraft.** A swap to a different type can change hold access entirely, and occasionally removes it.
- **The policy.** Carriers revise their pet conditions more often than travellers expect — the reason we keep a page per airline and why our guide on [airline pet policies changing](/travel-hub/airline-pet-policy-changes/) exists at all.

A check three or four days before departure costs five minutes and is the single most useful habit in this whole process.

Once the flight is secured, the rest follows a schedule rather than a scramble.
