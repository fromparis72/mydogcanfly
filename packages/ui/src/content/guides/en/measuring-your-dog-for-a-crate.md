Crate sizing is where most hold bookings go wrong, and it goes wrong in a very specific way: the owner measures the dog lying down, buys a crate that looks generous, and discovers at the desk that the animal cannot stand up inside it with its head raised. That is a refusal, not a negotiation.

The measurement itself takes five minutes. What follows is what to measure, how, and what to do with the numbers.

## The rule behind the numbers

Every airline applies some version of the same requirement, drawn from the IATA Live Animals Regulations: inside the crate, the dog must be able to **stand with its head up, turn around completely, and lie down naturally**, without touching the sides or roof.

Everything else — the four measurements, the margins — exists to satisfy that sentence. Our guide to [choosing an airline-approved crate](/travel-hub/airline-approved-dog-crate/) covers the construction requirements: ventilation on several sides, secure fastenings, a leak-proof floor.

## The four measurements

Take them on a **standing** dog, on a flat surface, with a soft tape measure.

1. **Length (A).** From the tip of the nose to the base of the tail — the base, not the tip. This is the measurement people most often take too short.
2. **Height at the shoulder (B).** From the ground to the top of the shoulder blade. Used with A to derive the required length.
3. **Standing height (C).** From the ground to the top of the head, or to the top of the ears if they stand erect. An erect-eared dog needs a taller crate than its shoulder height suggests.
4. **Width (D).** The widest point of the body, usually across the shoulders or hips.

Then the usual margins: the crate length is normally **A + half of B**, the internal height at least **C plus a few centimetres**, and the internal width at least **twice D** so the dog can turn.

Rather than doing that arithmetic by hand, put your measurements into our [travel crate calculator](/tools/crate/) — it returns the internal dimensions to look for, which is the figure to quote when you contact the airline.

## Why bigger is not safer

The instinct to round up is understandable and often wrong. Three reasons:

- **Movement.** A dog with too much space is thrown against the walls during loading, taxiing and turbulence. A correctly sized crate lets the animal brace.
- **Airline maximums.** Carriers have upper limits on crate dimensions, set by the hold door and the compartment. Exceeding them removes the flight, not just the crate.
- **Cost bracket.** On many carriers the price step is driven by the crate's dimensions and total weight.

Aim for the dog's measurements plus the standard margins — not for the largest crate that fits in your car.

## Weight is a separate limit

Dimensions and weight are checked independently. Most airlines set a maximum for the **combined weight of dog plus crate**, and a large crate is itself heavy before the animal is in it. If you are close to a threshold, weigh the loaded crate at home rather than discovering the figure at the desk.

Where that combined figure sits also determines whether your booking is accompanied baggage or manifest cargo — two different contracts, set out in [cabin, excess baggage or manifest cargo](/travel-hub/cargo-vs-excess-baggage/).

## Small dogs: measure for the cabin instead

If your dog may fit under the seat, measure for the **cabin** first. The constraint there is the space under the seat in front, which varies by aircraft and by carrier, and soft carriers are generally preferred because they compress. See [the most comfortable carriers for small dogs](/travel-hub/small-dog-carriers/), our [cabin carrier selection](/tools/best-carriers/), and the comparison of formats in [carriers and crates](/travel-hub/dog-carriers-and-crates/).

The cabin removes seasonal embargoes from the equation almost entirely — a substantial advantage explained in [summer heat embargoes](/travel-hub/pet-heat-embargoes/).

## Buy early, and let the dog live in it

A crate bought the week before departure is a crate the dog meets for the first time on the worst possible day. Buy it early, leave it open at home with a familiar blanket inside, feed the dog near it and then in it, and build up to closed-door sessions and short car trips. Weeks, not days.
