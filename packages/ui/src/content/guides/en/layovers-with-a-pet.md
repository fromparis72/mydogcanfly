Most pet itineraries that fail do not fail at departure. They fail at the connection. A stopover is where a second airline's rules can apply, where a second temperature threshold has to be cleared, where a delay turns into a missed loading, and where an animal is handled twice more than it would be on a direct flight.

If you take one thing from this guide: when a direct flight exists on your route, the case for paying for it is much stronger with a pet than without one.

## What a stopover actually adds

Four things, and they are independent of each other.

- **A temperature check.** Heat and cold embargoes apply at every airport on the itinerary. A mild departure and a mild arrival do not save you if the connection is above the threshold at that hour — see [summer heat embargoes](/travel-hub/pet-heat-embargoes/).
- **A second rulebook**, if the second leg is operated by another carrier. Two airlines can hold genuinely incompatible positions on breed, crate size or hold acceptance. Compare them on our [airline pet policy pages](/airlines/).
- **A handling operation.** Unloading, holding, reloading — each with its own timing.
- **A dependency on the first leg.** A delay that would merely annoy you can put the animal outside the connection window.

## Minimum connection time is not the passenger figure

Every airport publishes a minimum connection time. That figure is calculated for people and suitcases. Live animals frequently need **longer**, because they are handled separately and held in a dedicated facility between flights.

Some carriers publish a pet-specific minimum; some refuse to accept a pet on a connection below a threshold; some assess it case by case. Ask for the figure explicitly when you book, following the sequence in [how to book a flight with your dog](/travel-hub/booking-a-pet-flight/) — and get the answer in writing with the rest of the confirmation.

A related trap: a connection that requires **changing terminals**. Moving an animal between terminals is a different operation from moving it between gates, and at some airports it is not offered at all within a short window.

## The two-ticket problem

Booking two separate tickets to save money is a common strategy, and with a pet it is a poor one. On separate tickets there is no through-checking: you collect the animal, clear immigration and customs with it, then check it in again for a flight whose airline may apply different rules. On an international connection that can be impossible in the time available, and in some jurisdictions it means formally importing the animal only to export it again hours later.

If the itinerary must involve two tickets, treat the two legs as two separate journeys, each with its own paperwork, and allow a very generous margin — often an overnight.

## Cabin changes the picture entirely

A dog that travels under the seat stays with you throughout the stopover. No animal-handling facility, no reloading, no second temperature check that concerns it, and you can use a relief area between flights. Many large airports now provide them: our [airport pet relief area finder](/tools/pet-relief/) lists them by airport, and our [airport pages](/airports/) cover the practical detail terminal by terminal.

If your dog is close to the size limit, this is one more reason to measure carefully for the cabin rather than defaulting to the hold — see [the most comfortable carriers for small dogs](/travel-hub/small-dog-carriers/) and [measuring your dog for a crate](/travel-hub/measuring-your-dog-for-a-crate/).

## Building an itinerary that survives a delay

A few habits that cost little:

1. **Direct first.** Our [destination finder](/tools/destinations/) prioritises direct routes precisely for this reason.
2. **One carrier**, or at least one alliance with a single set of pet conditions.
3. **A long connection** rather than a tight one — with an animal, three hours is not excessive.
4. **Early departures**, which are less exposed to the day's accumulated delays and to afternoon heat.
5. **A plan B written down**: the next flight, and the carrier's own contact for pet bookings.

And re-check the whole itinerary a few days out. Operating carriers and aircraft change, and with them the pet conditions — the argument developed in [why airline pet policies change](/travel-hub/airline-pet-policy-changes/).
