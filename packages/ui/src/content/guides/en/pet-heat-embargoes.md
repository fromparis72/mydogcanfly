Every summer the same conversation repeats at check-in desks: a booking made months earlier, a crate that fits, paperwork in order — and an animal that cannot be loaded, because the temperature that day is above the airline's limit. This is a **heat embargo**, and it is neither arbitrary nor negotiable at the desk.

Understanding how embargoes are measured is what lets you plan around them. Almost every avoidable failure comes from checking the wrong airport.

## What an embargo actually is

Airlines carry animals in a pressurised, temperature-controlled hold compartment. In flight, conditions are manageable. The exposure happens **on the ground**: the wait in the animal-handling area, the trip across the apron, the time in the hold before the doors close and after they open at the other end.

Apron surfaces get far hotter than the air, and an aircraft sitting in the sun heats up quickly. So carriers set thresholds — a maximum above which they will not load an animal, and usually a minimum for cold — and apply them across the itinerary.

The ground-heat problem is the same one that burns paw pads at home, on a bigger scale: see [protecting paw pads from hot pavement](/travel-hub/hot-pavement-dog-paws/) for the everyday version, and [dog heatstroke](/travel-hub/dog-heatstroke/) for what overheating actually does to a dog.

## The rule that catches people out

The threshold does not apply to your city. It applies to **every airport your pet passes through**.

That means a comfortable morning in northern Europe is irrelevant if the connection is in a hot climate at midday, or if the arrival airport is above the limit when you land. Travellers routinely check their departure, see a mild forecast, and are surprised at the desk.

Two consequences worth acting on:

- **A direct flight is not merely more comfortable — it removes an entire point of failure.** One airport to clear instead of three.
- **The time of day matters as much as the date.** Early-morning and night departures are the standard way through a hot season.

## Heat is not the only seasonal limit

Cold thresholds exist too, and they bite in winter on northern routes. They are less discussed simply because fewer people fly pets in January, but the mechanism is identical — see [cold exposure and hypothermia in dogs](/travel-hub/dog-hypothermia/) for what low temperatures do to an animal that is not equipped for them.

And embargoes stack with breed rules. A snub-nosed dog can face a year-round hold refusal *and* a seasonal embargo on top: our guide to [snub-nosed breeds and flying](/travel-hub/snub-nosed-breeds-and-flying/) sets out that combination.

## Planning around an embargo

In order of effectiveness:

1. **Fly the animal in the cabin** if size allows. Embargoes essentially do not apply — see [the most comfortable carriers for small dogs](/travel-hub/small-dog-carriers/).
2. **Choose the month.** Shifting a trip by a few weeks often moves it out of the window entirely.
3. **Choose the hour.** Night and early-morning departures, at every airport on the route.
4. **Choose a direct flight**, to remove the stopover from the calculation.
5. **Consider manifest cargo**, where the carrier's cargo division operates under different handling arrangements — see [cabin, excess baggage or manifest cargo](/travel-hub/cargo-vs-excess-baggage/).

Then verify with the carrier, on your dates. Thresholds and their application vary between airlines, which is why we keep a page per carrier: compare them in our [airline pet policies](/airlines/), and re-check close to departure for the reasons in [why airline pet policies change](/travel-hub/airline-pet-policy-changes/).

## If your flight is embargoed at the last minute

It can be declared at short notice, on the day. Ask the airline what alternatives exist on the same booking — a later departure, a different routing, cargo — before rebooking independently, and keep the animal cool and hydrated while you sort it out. The one thing not to do is wait it out on an apron-side kerb in full sun with the dog in a crate.
