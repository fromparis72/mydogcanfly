The airport is where preparation becomes visible. Everything that was abstract — the crate dimensions, the paperwork, the booking confirmation — gets checked by someone in the space of a few minutes, and any gap shows up there rather than at home.

This guide follows the day in order, so you know what is coming and where the time actually goes.

## Before you leave home

Three things belong in your hand luggage, not in a suitcase:

- **The paperwork**, in the order it will be asked for: booking confirmation showing the animal, health documents, microchip number. See [pet travel documents by destination](/travel-hub/pet-travel-documents/), and build the file with our [travel sheet tool](/tools/fiche/).
- **Water and a collapsible bowl**, plus something absorbent — see [portable feeding gear](/travel-hub/portable-dog-feeding-gear/).
- **A lead**, kept accessible. You will need it the moment the carrier is opened.

Feed lightly and early. A dog that eats a full meal an hour before departure is more likely to be sick, a subject covered in [motion sickness in dogs](/travel-hub/dog-motion-sickness/).

## Check-in: a manual process

Pets are rarely handled by online check-in. Expect a specific desk, a queue, and a member of staff who will look at the animal as well as the documents. What they check, in roughly this order: that the booking shows the pet, that the paperwork matches the destination's requirements, and — for a hold animal — that the crate complies and that the dog can stand, turn and lie down inside it.

That last check is where crates fail on the day. If yours was sized with our [crate calculator](/tools/crate/) and the method in [measuring your dog for a crate](/travel-hub/measuring-your-dog-for-a-crate/), it is a formality. If it was bought by eye, it may not be.

Hold animals are handed over at this point, usually to an oversized-baggage or animal-handling position rather than the belt.

## Security screening with a cabin pet

The procedure is consistent almost everywhere: the **carrier goes through the X-ray empty**, and you carry the dog through the walk-through detector, on a lead. Some airports provide a screened room; most do not.

Two things make this go smoothly, and both are practised at home rather than learned in the queue: a dog that accepts being picked up and held calmly, and a dog that will go back into its carrier on request. Our guide to [dog travel anxiety](/travel-hub/dog-travel-anxiety/) covers that groundwork.

Keep the lead on until the carrier is closed again. An airside dog off-lead is a serious incident, not a small mistake.

## Between security and boarding

This is the part travellers plan least and need most. Find the **relief area** — many large airports now have one airside as well as landside, but provision is uneven and signage worse. Our [pet relief area finder](/tools/pet-relief/) lists them by airport, and our [airport pages](/airports/) cover the terminal-level detail.

Offer water. Avoid the hottest, busiest concourse if you have a choice: a quiet gate area is better for a dog than a food court. If you are connecting, this is also the moment to re-read the second leg's timing — see [layovers and connections with a pet](/travel-hub/layovers-with-a-pet/).

## Boarding and the flight itself

In the cabin, the carrier goes under the seat in front of you for taxi, take-off and landing, and normally stays there for the flight. Crew will tell you what is permitted on that aircraft; the answer varies more than people expect.

Keep expectations modest: a quiet dog that sleeps is the good outcome, and it is mostly a product of preparation rather than anything you can do in the air. Do not give any sedative that has not been prescribed for this purpose by a veterinarian who knows the animal — sedation at altitude carries risks of its own.

## On arrival

Collect a hold animal at the oversized-baggage or animal desk rather than the belt, and open the crate only once you are somewhere secure with the lead attached. Expect a dog that needs water, a walk and some quiet more than it needs enthusiasm.

If you have landed abroad, the destination's formalities happen here, and they are set by the arrival country rather than the airline — see our [country guides](/countries/) and [international travel with a dog](/travel-hub/international-travel-with-a-dog/).
